What changed our reviewer was not the model but how we split the work: 15 review cards, six models

2026-11-13 · Tam · Version 1.0

Tech Report 6: which model should run our review agent? We replayed 15 cards whose answers we knew, and the biggest lever turned out to be the unit of input, not the model.

See the one-card resultSee the false positives

Key findings

  • To decide which model should run BT, our team's review agent, we gave 15 real review cards to six model setups as the same input. The answer key is 12 findings from Dex, a teammate who reviewed the same cards first, that we confirmed against our records.
  • When we put all 15 cards in at once, Claude Sonnet caught 5 of the 12 (plus 4 partial). When we sent the same Sonnet one card at a time, it caught 9 (plus 2 partial). Same model, different way of feeding it.
  • One card at a time, Sonnet scored 9+2 and Opus scored 10+2. The gap sits inside the run-to-run variation we saw when we ran Sonnet three times (9 to 10), so we treat the two as equal and picked Sonnet, which costs about half as much.
  • There was a price. We sorted every Sonnet finding one by one. Sending one card at a time raised the false positive rate among findings we could judge from 5% to 22%, and raised "needs outside context" findings, which require files outside the card, from 17 to 51. Fewer misses, more work for the humans who filter.
  • The three Gemini models answered "none" on all 31 answers in the all-at-once run. We read this as a failure of how we fed them, not of the models' ability (footnote 1).

The starting question: can we trust "the reviewer found nothing"?

At Carvit, agents write documents and code, and other agents review them. Dex handles fact checking and rebuttals. BT handles new proposals and risk discovery. One day BT answered "none" several times in a row. Was there really no problem, or did BT miss it? A reviewer's "none" is the hardest answer to verify. So we decided to retest on a problem where we already knew the answer.

Experiment design

  • Input: 15 cards asking for review of 3 real changes (about 107,000 bytes). This is a consistency review of internal operations documents. Examples: a contradiction inside one document where "this one file is the source of truth" and "the status source of truth is that other file" appear together, and a sentence that states an upcoming schedule as settled fact.
  • Answer key of 12: We removed 3 of the 15 findings Dex made on the same cards. The removal rule is one: if a finding does not match the records, we remove it. For 2 of them, we opened the decision record and the original text turned out to be correct, so the "contradiction" was not one. For 1, Dex had called the same figure "correct" on a different card, so Dex disagreed with Dex. These 3 are not real problems, so if any model flagged them, we count that as a false positive, not a hit. If you keep the denominator at 15, the hit counts below stay the same and only the rates drop (Sonnet one card at a time: 9/12 becomes 9/15).
  • Models: Gemini 3.6 flash, 3.8 flash, and 3.1 pro preview, GPT-OSS 120B, Claude Sonnet and Opus.
  • Two ways of feeding the input: all 15 cards in one prompt, or one card at a time across 15 calls.
  • Unified conditions: Every model got the same card text and the same one-line system sentence, and no tools. We called Claude with project instructions switched off so they did not load. All numbers in the tables are under this condition.
  • Grading: For each of the 12 answer key items, hit, partial, or miss (recall). We collected findings outside the answer key separately and sorted them into valid, false positive, or duplicate (precision).

Result 1: when we put everything in at once

(GPT-OSS 120B was tested only in the one-card-at-a-time method, through a different call path. Its result is in the Result 2 table.)

All 31 of the Gemini answers were "none." This is exactly the scene we feared. If the reviewer loses track of what to look at on each card inside a long input, it answers "none." The two Claude models also stopped at 5 of 12.

Result 2: when we send one card at a time

The same Sonnet went from 5+4 to 9+2, and the same Opus went from 5+4 to 10+2. The model and the instructions stayed the same. Only the unit of input changed. One card is 3,000 to 10,000 tokens, so everything the reviewer must look at fits in one view. We read this result less as "the model was weak" and more as "we gave it too big a job."

Did false positives go up?

Yes. When you send one card at a time, the reviewer may feel it has to flag something on every card, so hits can look higher than they are. So we pulled out every finding in Sonnet's answers for both methods, line by line. Dex, a teammate who is not in the Claude family, compared each one against the original text of the paired card and sorted it.

(When one problem spans several cards, several lines match a single answer key item. A line that caught an item we removed from the answer key was counted as a false positive.)

Among findings that can be judged from the card alone, false positives were 5% all at once (2 of 38) and 22% one card at a time (12 of 54). The total of correct findings rose only a little, from 36 to 42. The real gain from one card at a time is missing fewer of the important problems in the answer key. The cost is that false positives that humans must filter grew 6 times (2 to 12), and "needs outside context" findings grew 3 times (17 to 51).

We accepted this trade. A problem missed in review gets more expensive later, while a reader can discard a false positive in a few seconds. Still, we read the 41% share of "needs outside context" as a sign that the reviewer wants information from outside the card, and in the next test we plan to try an instruction that collects those findings separately. Dex did this sorting once, and the lines Tam checked directly against the original text number two.

Variation and the grader

  • Repeats: We ran Sonnet one card at a time twice more on the same input (0 failed cards). The three runs scored 9, 9, and 10 hits. By the blind regrade, hits plus partials per run are 10, 9, and 11.
  • Blind regrade: With the run labels hidden, a different grader regraded the three answers. It matched the first grading on 11 of 12 items, and the one that differed sat on the boundary between partial and miss.
  • The type everyone missed: A finding that a notation joining a number and an item name could read differently to a reader was a miss in all three runs and for both graders. It asks about the chance of a reader's misunderstanding, not whether a sentence is wrong, so it is the hardest kind.
  • Grader bias disclosure: The hit grader is in the Claude family. Because it may prefer its own family, we narrowed the grading standard to matching against the answer key sentences, and we gave the false positive sorting to Dex, who is from a different family.

Cost and time

  • We state cost in API-equivalent dollars for 15 cards one card at a time (we ran within our subscription limit, and the numbers are the equivalent values reported by the calling tool). Sonnet is $0.73 and Opus is $1.43. All at once cost $0.58 for Sonnet and $1.03 for Opus.
  • One card at a time sends 4 cards concurrently, so for Sonnet the whole run finishes in the two-minute range (the longest card took 65 seconds). All at once took about four minutes. Review is work that can wait, so a miss costs more than speed.
  • Opus caught 1 more than Sonnet and cost about twice as much. The gap sits inside the repeat variation (9 to 10), so we did not treat it as a quality difference.

What we chose and what we changed

What the numbers told us goes only as far as "Sonnet and Opus are equal on this job, and Sonnet is cheaper." Any choice beyond that was the founder's judgment. The founder judged that, in a reviewer, the diligence to read the given card to the end matters more than broad talent, and that judgment is not a value measured by this experiment's tables. On the same day we changed BT's default path.

  • If a card header shows two or more cards, split them and send one card at a time, 4 at once.
  • If even one card gets an answer, count it as success. If all fail, pass to the next path.
  • Keep the old path so we can swap in a new model by retesting on the same 15 cards.
CARD_RE = re.compile(r"(?m)^(?==+ ?카드 )")

def split_cards(text):
    parts = CARD_RE.split(text)
    cards = [p for p in parts if re.match(r"=+ ?카드 ", p)]
    if len(cards) < 2:
        return text, []          # a single card goes through as is
    head = "" if re.match(r"=+ ?카드 ", parts[0]) else parts[0]
    return head, cards           # shared preamble + the cards

with ThreadPoolExecutor(4) as ex:  # 4 cards at a time
    answers = list(ex.map(review_one, [head + ONE + c for c in cards]))

Limitations

  • The answer key started from Dex's findings. A real problem that Dex did not see does not count toward the hit score, and shows up only as "valid" in the false positive sorting.
  • It is 15 cards, 12 answers, and one kind of review (consistency of operations documents). Extending it to code review or security review may give different results.
  • We used the same short instruction for every model. Tuning the instruction per model might change the scores, and we did not run that test.
  • We ran 3 repeats only for Sonnet one card at a time. We do not know the variation of the other setups.

What we did not do

  • Per-model instruction tuning, public benchmarks, and multiple human graders. Our question was "which model reads our review cards diligently," and for that, our own cards were the most accurate test paper.
  • We do not publish the card text or the documents under review, because they are internal operations documents.

Five things you can do right away

  1. If the reviewer's "none" keeps coming up, retest on a past case where you know the answer.
  2. Do not hand over a big batch all at once. Split it by review unit (card or file) and send one at a time. This is worth trying before swapping models. But false positives and "needs outside context" findings will increase, so prepare to read with a filter.
  3. Check the answer key against records and remove wrong findings first. Write down the removal rule and also the score on the original denominator.
  4. Run the same setup three times to see the variation, and if the gap is inside the variation, pick the cheaper one.
  5. Do not count only hits. Count false positives too. If the grader is a model of the same family, hide the labels for a blind grade or hand it to a different family, and say so in the write-up.
