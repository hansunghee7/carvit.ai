Why a pricier model rarely looked different for us: what our agent team measured

2026-11-21 · Version 1.0

Tech Report 8: we counted how often our agents actually call a top-tier model, then looked at five of our own measurements. On our work, the pricier model rarely looked different. We do not yet know why, and we say what we will test next.

See the five measurementsSee the limits

Key findings

  • We counted our agent team's sub-agent calls: 2,110 in total. Sonnet took 1,295, calls that inherited the parent session's model took 663, Opus 87, the top tier above Opus 62, and Haiku 3. The top two tiers add up to 149 calls, about 7%. We did not set out to save money. Most of the time the work simply did not ask for them.
  • On lookups and small edits, 8 tasks run 3 times each (24 runs per model), Opus scored 24/24, Sonnet 23/24 and Haiku 24/24. The one Sonnet miss was a grader that accepted one spelling of "no" and not another. Haiku cost 5.6% of Opus; Sonnet cost 88.1%.
  • On multi-step tasks the picture changed for the smallest model: Haiku scored 19/24 and Sonnet 24/24. So "send light work to the small model" held for one-step lookups and failed for multi-step work. These tasks are synthetic and Opus was not in this run.
  • On a merge-review job over 148 problem PRs and 60 controls, Opus flagged 32.4% of the problem PRs with a 20.0% false alarm rate, Sonnet 8.1% with 8.3%, Haiku 34.5% with 23.3%. None reached the bar we fixed beforehand (false alarms at most 25% and detection at least 50%). We found no evidence that the larger model was the better reviewer here.
  • A one-line "stay within the scope of the task" instruction took scope creep from 35 to 45% down to 0/40 on all three models. Rewriting a synthetic set of documents with headings, tables and links lifted Haiku from 58.8% to 86.4%, close to Opus on the plain version (90.5%). All of these are our own small samples, not general laws.
  • We then ran the test we had planned: 7 past problems, Sonnet or Opus, current or minimal instructions, 3 runs each, 84 calls with no errors. Correct first answers out of 21 per setup: Opus with current instructions 9, Opus with minimal 13, Sonnet with current 11, Sonnet with minimal 13. Against the bars we fixed beforehand, the claim that instruction load is the limit was not supported, and Opus added no more than 10 points under either set of instructions. Only 2 of the 7 problems separated the setups, so we read this as a direction and not a result.

The starting question: why did the pricier model not look different?

Our agents are given a choice of model every time they hand work to a sub-agent. We kept hearing, and believing, that a stronger model is worth its price. In daily work we mostly could not see it. Tasks that went to a top-tier model did not feel different from the same tasks on a cheaper one. A feeling is not evidence in either direction, so we measured.

This is not the same question as Tech Report 6. That report compared six setups for one review job and found the way we fed the input mattered more than the model. Here we step back and ask across our whole workload: when does a bigger model earn its place, and when does it not?

What we counted first

Before measuring anything we counted what our agents actually did. In our agent team's session logs, sub-agent calls totalled 2,110:

Opus and the tier above it add up to 149 of 2,110, about 7%. The 663 inherited calls are a blind spot: this count cannot say which model they ran on, so about 7% is the share of calls where a top-tier model was named. We read it as a lower bound for the share and not as a measured fact about cost.

Five measurements

Each of these was built to answer a narrow question about our own work. None was designed together, and the samples are small. Read each as "what happened here", not as a ranking of models.

Lookups and small edits (N1)

We gave 8 tasks of reading our documents and making one small edit to Opus, Sonnet and Haiku, 3 runs each, 24 runs per model.

Sonnet's single miss was not a wrong answer: the content was right, but the grader's pattern accepted one Korean spelling of "no" and not the other. Haiku took slightly more turns but did not fail. For this size of job the small model matched the big one at about one eighteenth of the cost. Sonnet, the middle model, barely saved anything here. The Opus control run was measured about an hour earlier and its exact model ID was not recorded, and the tasks are mostly one-step lookups where everyone scores near full marks, so this measurement says little about harder work.

Multi-step tasks (K2)

We then asked whether the same logic holds when a task needs several steps. We fixed the pass rule before running: Haiku passes if it is within 3 of Sonnet and costs at most half. We ran 8 synthetic tasks (4 types, 2 seeds each) 3 times per model.

Five Haiku failures: three arithmetic slips on one task that sums values it has just looked up, and two on another where it dropped a required prefix from its answer. The gap was 5, past the limit of 3, so the pre-fixed rule says reject. Even if the two formatting misses were forgiven, the gap would be 3 at a cost of 60%, still not a pass. Opus was not part of this run, and eight synthetic tasks with most failures coming from one of them is a thin basis.

Judging whether a PR should merge (C1)

This is the job where we most expected the top tier to help. Given a change, can a model say "concerns" or "no concerns", and does that match what later happened to the change? We defined problem PRs by what happened next: a later change deleted or rewrote lines the PR added, or the PR was reverted. That gave 148 problem PRs, set against 60 controls. Each PR was judged 3 times per model, and a PR counts as flagged if at least 2 of 3 judgments raised a concern. We fixed the bar first: false alarms at most 25% and detection at least 50%.

No model met both conditions, and the verdict for all three was "partial". Opus and Haiku found about a third of the problem PRs and were wrong on roughly one control in five. Sonnet raised few alarms and found few. Giving Opus a longer view of the change (a larger cap on how much of the change it saw) moved the numbers by almost nothing. We found no evidence in this job that the larger model reviews better. Note that our definition of "problem PR" is a proxy: some later edits are just ongoing updates to a tracking document and not defects.

One line of instruction (S2)

If a model drifts beyond the task it was given, does a stronger model stop? We wrote 20 tasks and wrapped each with prompts that invited the agent to tidy up nearby things. Half the runs added one line telling the agent to stay within the task's scope. That is 80 sessions per model, 40 per condition.

Every model drifted without the line, the stronger model slightly less, and none drifted with it. Task success was 100% in all six cells, so the tasks themselves did not tell the models apart. In this one, a sentence in the instructions did what a bigger model did not. The prompts were written to invite drift, so the rates are an upper bound and not a natural rate, and we used one random seed.

How the documents are structured (S4h3)

We asked the models questions about a synthetic body of facts: 217 facts across 24 projects, with 128 questions, many about which of several changed values is current. We wrote the same facts three ways. "Plain" is ordinary prose. "Ledger" is the same prose with the newest value placed first and marked "(current)". "Structured" adds headings, tables and links. Each question was asked 5 times.

Scores are percent correct. Putting structure around the same facts lifted Haiku by about 28 points, to within about 4 points of Opus reading the plain version. The ledger form alone did nothing for Haiku. For Opus, which is near the ceiling, structure and ledger form each helped a little. Our pre-fixed rule needed both models to agree on direction, so the formal verdict was "unclear". What we can say is that how the document is organised moved the small model's score more than the model class did. The facts are synthetic, in one writing style, from one set, and Sonnet was not run.

Next to what Anthropic says

Anthropic's engineering team describes the model's attention as a limited budget and recommends giving it the smallest set of high-signal information it needs, loading the rest when it is needed instead of all at once (Effective context engineering for AI agents). Its research team recommends starting with the simplest solution and adding complexity only when it demonstrably helps, and describes routing work to specialised paths as a pattern for exactly this (Building effective agents).

Our measurements point in the same direction as those two pieces, and we want to be careful about how far. They are guidance written for builders in general. Our numbers are small tests of our own setup. We did not test their claims, and our results do not prove them. What the two do give us is a reason to suspect that the way a job is shaped and fed to a model can matter as much as which model gets it.

Our hypothesis, the test we ran, and what comes next

Hypothesis: much of our work may already be in a form that does not need a top-tier model. It is bounded, such as a lookup or a small edit, judged against rules, such as a merge check, or handed over with structure and a clear instruction. The five measurements above do not show this. They leave it open. We also suspected that long, dense instructions may leave a strong model less room to reason. We do not claim our instructions are the problem.

The test (M1), with the rules fixed before we saw any result. We took 7 real past problems where the cause was unknown or the design could go two ways, and asked Sonnet and Opus to find the cause under two instruction sets: our current full instructions, and a minimal set that keeps only the hard rules. Each of the 4 setups ran each problem 3 times: 84 calls, 0 errors, about $11.7 in API-equivalent cost. The models could only read.

The two bars we set beforehand: if Opus with minimal instructions beats Sonnet with current instructions by 25 points or more, we would take it as support for "instruction load, not the model, is the limit". If the gain from Opus is under 10 points under both instruction sets, we would take it as "little benefit from the top tier on our work". The first bar was not met: the gap was 10 points (62% against 52%). The second was met: Opus minus Sonnet was minus 9 points under current instructions and 0 under minimal. So the claim that instruction load is the limit is not supported by this test, and the top tier added little on these problems.

Two things we did not set a bar for, so read them as exploration only. Cutting the instructions to the minimal set was followed by 10 more correct points for Sonnet and 19 for Opus. And the picture is thinner than the table looks. Three of the 7 problems were answered correctly in all 12 runs, two were missed in all 12, and only the other two moved: on one, Sonnet with minimal instructions was right 3 times in 3 and Opus with current instructions 0 in 3, and on the other only Opus with minimal instructions was right 3 times in 3.

What comes next: a test of lighter instructions on their own, since that is the one lever that moved in the right direction for both models. We will fix the bars first again, and we will use more problems that can separate the setups.

Limitations

  • The counts of 7% and 2,110 come from our own session logs. The 663 inherited calls cannot be assigned to a model.
  • Every measurement above is our own, small and built for a narrow question: 24 runs per model for N1 and K2, 148 problem PRs for C1, one seed for S2, one set of synthetic facts for S4h3. The effects are not claims about other teams or other work.
  • N1, K2 and S4h3 use synthetic tasks or facts, so they do not carry the ambiguity of real work. C1 is real PR history, but "problem PR" is our proxy.
  • The five measurements used different tasks, graders and, in places, different model subsets (Opus is missing from K2, Sonnet from S4h3). They do not add up into one ranking of models.
  • The M1 test used 7 problems, 3 runs per setup and one set of minimal instructions. Three problems were answered correctly by every setup and two were missed by every setup, so only two problems told the setups apart. The sample is small, and the differences are directions and not findings.
  • About half of the M1 answers (37 of 84) were ambiguous and were graded by a Sonnet reviewer who did not see which model or instructions produced them. It belongs to the same family as two of the four setups, so it may favor them. The other 47 were graded by keyword rules. The models could only read, and the effort setting was the default.

What we did not do

  • A comparison with public benchmarks, or with other vendors' models.
  • Human grading of the merge-review judgments, or a second grader for the open-ended parts.
  • We do not publish the tasks, documents or PR contents because they are internal.

Four things you can do right away

  1. Count first. Look at how often your agents really call the top tier before debating whether it is worth it.
  2. Do not assume the small model fails on light work or the big model wins on hard work. Run one small test for each job type you actually have, with the pass rule written down beforehand.
  3. Before swapping the model, try the cheap levers: one sentence about scope, structure and headings in the documents it reads, and feeding the job in smaller pieces (see Tech Report 6).
  4. Keep the jobs where you cannot yet tell separate and test them on their own, with problems that can actually separate the setups. In our hard-problem test, 5 of 7 problems told us nothing about the difference.
